How long do home appliances last?

Updated September 8, 2026

Knowing roughly how long things last is useful for two decisions: whether to repair or replace something that has broken, and how much to budget for the things that have not broken yet but will.

The figures below are typical ranges drawn from widely cited industry estimates. Any individual unit can beat or miss them substantially depending on build quality, how hard it is used, water hardness, and whether it was maintained at all.

What actually shortens these

  • Hard water — brutal on water heaters, dishwashers, washing machines and kettles. In a hard-water area, expect the lower end of every range unless you soften or descale.
  • Skipped maintenance — an unflushed water heater or an unserviced furnace can lose years.
  • Restricted airflow — clogged furnace filters and blocked dryer vents make the equipment work harder for less output.
  • Overloading — consistently stuffing a washing machine wears bearings and suspension early.
  • Heavy use — a household of five runs a dishwasher three times as often as a couple, and the lifespan reflects that.

Repair or replace?

Two rules of thumb, used together:

The 50% rule

If the repair costs more than half the price of a new equivalent, replace it.

The lifespan rule

If the unit is past about 75% of its typical lifespan, lean towards replacing even for a cheaper repair — you are likely buying a short reprieve before the next failure.

A dishwasher at year three with a $200 repair is an easy fix. The same repair at year eleven is usually money spent twice.

Two things shift the maths. First, check whether it is still under warranty before paying for anything — people routinely pay for repairs on covered appliances because they cannot find the receipt and assume the worst. Second, on old heating, cooling and water-heating equipment, a new unit is often significantly more efficient, and the running cost difference is real money over the years you will own it.

Why the purchase date is worth recording

Every judgement on this page depends on knowing how old the thing is. Most people genuinely do not — "about five years?" turns out to be nine, or three. Recording the purchase date and price once, when you buy, means that years later the repair-or-replace decision is arithmetic rather than a guess, and that you never pay for a repair that a live warranty would have covered.
