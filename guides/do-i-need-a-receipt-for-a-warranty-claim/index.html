Do you need the receipt for a warranty claim?

Updated September 8, 2026

Short answer: usually yes, and a clear photo of the receipt is normally accepted. The warranty period is measured from the date of purchase, and the manufacturer has no way to know that date unless you can show it.

Why the receipt matters so much

A manufacturer's warranty is a promise about a specific unit for a specific length of time. To honour it, they need two facts: that you bought the item, and when. The serial number proves the first only loosely — it tells them when the unit was manufactured, not when it was sold.

That gap matters. A fridge might sit in a warehouse and on a shop floor for eight months before you buy it. Without a receipt, a manufacturer will often fall back on the manufacturing date plus a small allowance, and you can quietly lose months of coverage that you actually paid for.

What counts as proof of purchase

More things than most people realise. In rough order of how readily they are accepted:

  • The original itemised receipt — always the strongest.
  • A clear photo or scan of it — accepted by most manufacturers, and far more durable than the paper.
  • An order confirmation or invoice email from an online purchase.
  • Your account order history at the retailer, which often survives long after the email is deleted.
  • A credit card or bank statement line — proves the date and amount, but not what you bought, so it is usually a supporting document rather than a substitute.
  • A product registration completed at the time of purchase.

Thermal paper fades — usually before the warranty ends

This is the practical problem nobody plans for. Most till receipts are printed on thermal paper, which has no ink: the image is formed by heat darkening a coating. That coating keeps reacting to heat, light and friction for the rest of its life.

A thermal receipt left in a warm drawer can become unreadable within a year or two, and a receipt left in a car or a sunlit windowsill can go blank in months. A five-year appliance warranty backed by a thermal receipt is, in practice, backed by nothing after year two.

Photograph thermal receipts the day you get them. Not eventually — that day. It is the single highest-value habit in this entire article.

What to do when the receipt is gone

Not a lost cause. Work through these in order:

  1. Search your email for the retailer's name around the purchase date. Order confirmations survive far longer than paper.
  2. Check the retailer's online account. Many keep order history for years and can reprint a receipt, especially if you used a loyalty card or store card.
  3. Ask the store to look it up. If you paid by card, many retailers can find the transaction from the card number and date.
  4. Check whether you registered the product. If you did, the manufacturer already has your purchase date on file.
  5. Try the card statement. Combined with a serial number and a reasonable explanation, this is sometimes enough — especially for a clear manufacturing defect.

Register the product anyway

Product registration is often framed as a marketing exercise, and partly it is. But it also puts your purchase date in the manufacturer's own records, which is precisely the fact you will need later. For anything expensive with a multi-year warranty, it is five minutes well spent — and it means a lost receipt is an inconvenience rather than a dead end.

Keep the proof and the deadline together

The reason warranty claims turn into arguments is that the two pieces of information live apart. The receipt is in a drawer; the expiry date is in nobody's head. When the machine breaks you are hunting for paper while trying to remember whether you bought it two years ago or four.

Keeping the photo of the receipt attached to the warranty expiry date solves both problems at once. You get told before the coverage runs out, and when you do need to claim, the proof is already in your hand.
